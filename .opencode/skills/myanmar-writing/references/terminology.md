# Terminology and Code-Switching

Natural Burmese digital writing often mixes Burmese and English. Decide term by
term from audience familiarity, context, and brand practice. Do not target a
fixed percentage of English.

## Use the word the audience already uses

Natural code-switching is not limited to technical jargon. In many Myanmar
business and Facebook contexts, readers may normally say or write `website`,
`domain`, `link`, `post`, `page`, `position`, `salary`, `booking`, or
`appointment`. Keep such an English word when it is more immediate and familiar
for the intended audience than a formal or uncommon Burmese alternative.

This is a usage decision, not a fixed keep-list. The same word may stay English
in a salon recruitment post and become Burmese in an official notice. Prefer
evidence in this order: the user's requested wording, approved brand examples,
the audience's established usage, then the clearest natural option.

Use correct ordinary English spelling and capitalization. Fix an obvious typo
such as `postion` to `position` unless it is part of a locked name, identifier,
quotation, or the user explicitly requires that exact form. Do not copy every
English source word into the draft, and do not add English merely to make the
writing look modern.

## Keep the original form when accuracy depends on it

Keep these unchanged unless an official localized form is supplied:

- brand, company, product, model, and campaign names;
- usernames, URLs, email addresses, hashtags, and phone numbers;
- commands, code, file paths, flags, API fields, and UI labels being quoted;
- legal names and technical identifiers;
- specialist terms the intended readers normally search for or say in English.

Never invent a Burmese spelling merely to avoid English. An unfamiliar
transliteration is usually harder to understand than the established English
term.

This rule is strict for names and locations. Use a Burmese form only when it is
supplied by the user, established in approved brand samples, or verified from a
reliable source. Otherwise keep the source form rather than guessing. If the
brief says `Sanchaung`, preserve it until `စမ်းချောင်း` is supplied or verified;
never invent a different place name. The same applies to people, streets,
shops, courses, products, and campaigns. Proper names may be transliterated;
their meaning should not be translated.

## Prefer Burmese when it carries the idea naturally

Use ordinary Burmese for ordinary actions, relationships, benefits, and
explanations. English in the source is not a reason to retain English in the
draft. A general audience may prefer `ဝက်ဘ်ဆိုက်`, while a developer audience
may naturally use `website`; both can be right.

Ask these questions:

1. What would this audience call the thing in conversation?
2. Which form will they recognize or search for fastest?
3. Is the English word part of an official name or UI?
4. Does a Burmese form clarify the meaning, or only make it sound formal?
5. Has the user's existing brand copy already made a consistent choice?

Use that answer consistently within the piece unless the context changes.

## Attach Burmese grammar naturally

English nouns can sit inside a Burmese clause while Burmese particles and verbs
carry the grammar:

- `AI ကို ဘယ်နေရာမှာ သုံးသင့်လဲ`
- `Facebook မှာ post တင်မယ်`
- `Docker နဲ့ deploy လုပ်ထားတယ်`
- `website ကနေ စာရင်းသွင်းနိုင်ပါတယ်`

These are patterns, not a required vocabulary. Do not stack English words until
the clause loses its Burmese anchor. Conversely, do not translate a familiar
technical phrase into an explanation every time it appears.

## First-use explanations

Explain an unfamiliar term on first use only when the audience needs it. A
short Burmese explanation is often better than a dictionary-style parenthesis.
Do not gloss universal brand names or common terms merely because they are in
English.

## Capitalization and spelling

Use the official spelling for brands and products. Preserve meaningful case in
terms such as `OpenAI`, `YouTube`, `iPhone`, `Node.js`, and `ChatGPT`. Do not
apply decorative Unicode alphabets to headings; they reduce searchability and
accessibility.

When the source contains inconsistent spellings, choose one standard form and
flag the normalization only if it could affect identity or approval.

## Brief metadata is not terminology

Instructions such as "audience is existing customers", "sound warm", or "avoid
fake scarcity" control the writing. Do not turn them into lines such as
`ရှိပြီးသား customer တွေအတွက်ပါ` or mention that scarcity is not being used.
Only publish the offer and facts intended for readers.

## Numbers and units

Do not convert numbers by register. Preserve source facts exactly. Arabic digits
are a practical default for dates, prices, percentages, measurements, model
numbers, and web content. Use Burmese digits when the user's brand or requested
style calls for them. Never change a decimal separator, currency symbol, phone
number, date order, or unit without explicit reason.
